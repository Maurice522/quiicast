Good tutoring is visual. Walking through a math proof, annotating an essay, or stepping through a line of code is a lot easier to follow when the student can actually watch it happen instead of imagining it from a description over the phone. A tutor who can show their work, live, tends to get further in an hour than one limited to talking.

## Why dedicated classroom software is often overkill

Full e-learning platforms are great for scheduled classes with rosters and recordings, but for a one-off session — a single student, a single hour, a single topic — signing both people up for an account just to share a whiteboard is a lot of setup for not much payoff. It's worse when the student is young enough that creating an account requires a parent's involvement, or the tutor is working across a few different platforms depending on the client.

## Running the session

1. A tutor opens [quiicast.com/caster](/caster) and starts sharing their screen — a PDF, an IDE, a browser tab with practice problems, whatever the lesson needs.
2. They send the student the 4-digit code, ideally a minute before the scheduled start time so there's no fumbling once the lesson begins.
3. The student opens [quiicast.com/receiver](/receiver), enters the code, and is watching within seconds, on their laptop, tablet, or phone.
4. Audio for the actual lesson still runs over a phone call, video call, or whatever the tutor and student already use to talk — QuiiCast just handles the visual side.

Because it works in any modern browser, it doesn't matter what device the student has. And because there's no account on either side, there's no "I forgot my password" derailing the first five minutes of a session — a bigger deal than it sounds when a 30-minute lesson has already lost 5 minutes to logins before you've started.

## Making the most of it

- Use **Full screen** on the student's end so the shared material fills their screen without the tutor's browser chrome around it as a distraction.
- If you're annotating or writing live, a slightly lower frame-rate quality preset is usually indistinguishable for handwriting or text, and it's easier on a student's connection if they're on mobile data.
- For a small study pod rather than 1:1, the same code supports up to 5 students watching at once — useful for a quick group review session before a test.
- Keep a second window open with your lesson plan or notes on a *different* screen than the one you're sharing, so you're not accidentally exposing your prep notes to the student.

## FAQ

**Can the student write or draw on the shared screen too?**
Not directly — QuiiCast is one-way viewing from the tutor's screen. For a genuinely interactive whiteboard where both sides draw, you'd want a dedicated collaborative whiteboard tool running on the tutor's shared screen instead.

**What age of student does this work for?**
Any age that can type a 4-digit code and open a link — for younger children, a parent typically handles that part, and there's nothing further to sign up for after that.

**Is a session recorded anywhere?**
No — nothing is recorded or stored. Once the tab closes, the session is gone, which is worth knowing if a tutor wants to record a lesson for later review (that would need separate screen-recording software running locally).

It won't replace a full classroom platform for a term-long course, but for a single focused session, it's the fastest way to get two screens looking at the same thing.
