Live-coding interview platforms are useful when you want a shared, sandboxed editor. But sometimes what you actually want to see is how a candidate works in their *own* environment — their own editor, their own shortcuts, their own way of reading documentation — not a stripped-down browser code box.

## The case for watching, not co-editing

A shared coding sandbox is good for a specific kind of exercise. It's a worse fit when the role is about debugging a real, messy codebase, reasoning about a system design on a whiteboard app, or walking through an existing project the candidate brought with them. In those cases, you want to watch them work, ask questions, and let them drive.

## How QuiiCast fits

The candidate opens [quiicast.com/caster](/caster), shares their screen, and gives you the 4-digit code. You watch on [quiicast.com/receiver](/receiver) while they talk through their thinking — no interview platform to onboard them into, no plugin, no account setup eating into a 45-minute slot.

If you're interviewing with a co-interviewer, the same code supports both of you watching at once, so you don't need to relay what's happening on a second call.

## Things worth setting up beforehand

- Send the candidate the [receiver link](/receiver) in advance if *they'll* be watching something from you (a system diagram, a codebase to discuss), or the [caster link](/caster) if they'll be sharing.
- Confirm audio happens over your usual call tool — QuiiCast handles the screen, not the conversation.
- If the candidate is on a slower connection, suggest they pick a lower quality preset before starting; a crisp 480p feed of a terminal is far more useful than a stuttering 4K one.

It's a small piece of a good interview process, not a replacement for one — but for "let me just watch you work," it removes a surprising amount of setup friction on both sides.
