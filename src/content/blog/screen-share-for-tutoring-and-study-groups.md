Good tutoring and good study sessions share a requirement: everyone needs to be looking at the same thing, at the same time, while someone talks through it. A math proof, an annotated essay, a professor's slide deck — all of it lands better watched live than described over the phone.

## One tutor, one student

For a single focused session, signing both people up for a full e-learning platform just to share a whiteboard is a lot of setup for not much payoff — worse when the student is young enough that account creation needs a parent involved. A tutor opens [quiicast.com/caster](/caster), shares a PDF, an IDE, or a page of practice problems, and sends the student a 4-digit code. The student opens [quiicast.com/receiver](/receiver) and is watching within seconds, on whatever device they have. There's no account on either side, so there's no "I forgot my password" eating into the first five minutes.

A couple of things that help: use **Full screen** on the student's end so shared material fills their view without browser chrome around it, and if you're annotating live, a slightly lower frame-rate quality preset is usually indistinguishable for handwriting or text while being easier on a student's mobile connection.

## Study groups

![The tutor's actual sharing screen, showing the code to send the group](/blog/screenshot-caster-sharing.png)

The same setup works for a group: whoever has the best notes, the clearest summary, or the practice set everyone needs shares their screen, and the code goes in the group chat. Up to 5 people can join at once — enough for most study groups — and because there's no account required, everyone can be watching within a minute of the code being sent. That matters for the sessions that come together spontaneously, like the night before an exam, when nobody wants to schedule anything formally.

## What dedicated classroom platforms are for

Full e-learning and classroom platforms earn their setup cost for an ongoing, scheduled course: a roster, recorded sessions, assignments, a persistent classroom that exists week over week. For a single hour with one student, or a group that's meeting once before a test, that entire setup is overhead the session doesn't need. Reach for a real classroom platform once tutoring becomes a recurring, scheduled thing rather than a one-off — the tradeoff shifts once there's a term's worth of sessions to organize.

## FAQ

**Can the student write or draw on the shared screen too?** Not through QuiiCast — it's one-way viewing from the tutor's screen. For a genuinely interactive whiteboard both sides draw on, you'd want a dedicated collaborative whiteboard tool running on the tutor's shared screen instead.

**Does everyone need to create an account?** No — neither sharing a screen nor watching one requires signing up, on a school Chromebook or otherwise; nothing needs installing, so managed or restricted school devices generally work fine too.

**Is a session recorded?** No — nothing is stored, and once the tab closes the session is gone. A tutor wanting a recording for later review would need separate screen-recording software running locally.

It won't replace a full classroom platform for a term-long course, but for a single focused session — tutoring or a study group — it's the fastest way to get everyone looking at the same screen.
