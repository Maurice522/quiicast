Most screen-sharing tools are built as part of something bigger, and the "bigger" part is where the friction comes from: an installer, an account, a meeting to schedule, a plan to pick. If what you want is to show someone your screen in the next two minutes, the useful question isn't which tool has the most features. It's **what does each one demand from me, and from them, before the first frame appears?**

This page lists those requirements honestly. Product plans change, so treat the table as a starting point and confirm current terms on each vendor's site.

## What each option typically requires

| Option | Install | Account to start sharing | Control of the other machine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zoom | Desktop app recommended | Usually, to host | Optional remote control during a meeting |
| Google Meet | None, runs in the browser | Google account to host | No |
| Microsoft Teams | App or browser | Microsoft account to host | Optional control during a meeting |
| Chrome Remote Desktop | Chrome extension and host software | Google account | Yes, full control |
| TeamViewer / AnyDesk | App install on at least one side | Varies by plan | Yes, full control |
| QuiiCast | None | None | No, view-only |

Two patterns stand out. Meeting platforms need an account on the person who starts the session, though guests often get by without one. Remote-desktop tools need software on the machine being controlled, which is exactly what you can't ask of a relative who is already struggling.

## What "no download, no account" costs you

A browser-only share removes the setup, and in exchange it gives up features. Being straightforward about that matters, because the lightest tool is the wrong one when you need the features:

- **View-only.** The viewer can't click or type on your machine. For fixing a setting on someone else's computer, remote desktop is the right tool.
- **No chat, no recording, no participant list.** You talk over a call or a chat you already have open.
- **A handful of viewers.** Up to five people per code, built for small groups rather than a webinar.
- **Sending needs a capable browser.** Sharing a screen from a phone is limited by the platform, and iPhones can't send at all; see [how to screen share on iPhone](/blog/screen-share-on-iphone) for what works instead. Receiving works almost everywhere.

## How the lightest path works

![The cast page before sharing starts, with no sign-in field anywhere](/blog/screenshot-caster-idle.png)

The sender opens [quiicast.com/caster](/caster), clicks start, picks a screen, window or tab in the browser's own picker, and reads out a four-digit code. The viewer opens [quiicast.com/receiver](/receiver) and types it. Both sides use only the browser they already have, and the video goes directly between the two browsers instead of through a server. The [how it works](/how-it-works) page covers the connection and the [security page](/security) explains how the code is protected.

## When to choose something else

- **More than five viewers, or you need recordings and chat:** use a meeting platform.
- **You need to operate the other computer:** use a remote-desktop tool and accept the install.
- **Everyone is already in one app:** use that app's built-in sharing. It will be simpler than anything external.
- **Someone is on an iPhone and needs to send:** use FaceTime or a call app's broadcast option, not a browser.

![A viewer's browser connected to a live share, with nothing installed](/blog/screenshot-receiver-watching.png)

The right answer is rarely a single tool. Keep a meeting platform for meetings, a remote-desktop tool for hands-on fixes, and a browser-only share for the quick "look at this" moments that don't justify either.
