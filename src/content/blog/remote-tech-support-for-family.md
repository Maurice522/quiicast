The call always starts the same way: "the internet's not working" or "there's a weird popup" or "I can't find my email." You could talk them through it over the phone, describing where to click on a screen you can't see — or you could just look at the screen yourself.

## The problem with most remote-support tools

Most screen-sharing and remote-desktop tools assume the person on the other end can install software, create an account, or read a support ID off a dialog box under pressure. If your parent, grandparent, or sibling has ever frozen up trying to find "that download button," you know how badly this can go before you've even started fixing anything.

## How QuiiCast fits

QuiiCast skips the install entirely. Whoever has the problem opens [quiicast.com/caster](/caster) in whatever browser they already have open, clicks **Start sharing**, and reads you a 4-digit code over the phone. You type that into [quiicast.com/receiver](/receiver) on your own computer, and their screen appears on yours — no account, no download, nothing to explain twice.

Because the connection is peer-to-peer, you're seeing their actual screen in real time, so you can say "click the one that says Settings, top right" instead of guessing where the cursor probably is. If they need to move the mouse somewhere themselves, you can talk them there visually instead of over a phone held at arm's length.

## What to know before you start

- The code expires after a few minutes and stops working once the person closes the tab, so there's nothing left running afterward for them to worry about.
- If it's just diagnosing a settings issue, that's normally enough — you're watching, not controlling their computer.
- On a slow connection, dropping the quality preset (720p is usually still plenty readable) can make a laggy older laptop feel much smoother to watch.

Next time someone in your family says "can you just look at it," send them the [cast link](/caster) instead of driving over.
