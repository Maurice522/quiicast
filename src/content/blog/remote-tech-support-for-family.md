The call always starts the same way: "the internet's not working" or "there's a weird popup" or "I can't find my email." You could talk them through it over the phone, describing where to click on a screen you can't see — or you could just look at the screen yourself.

## The problem with most remote-support tools

Most screen-sharing and remote-desktop tools assume the person on the other end can install software, create an account, or read a support ID off a dialog box under pressure. If your parent, grandparent, or sibling has ever frozen up trying to find "that download button," you know how badly this can go before you've even started fixing anything. Some of these tools are also exactly what tech-support scam callers ask victims to install, which understandably makes cautious family members hesitant to install anything a relative asks them to, even when it's legitimate.

## Walking them through it, step by step

1. Tell them to open their normal web browser — Chrome, Safari, Edge, whatever they already use.
2. Have them go to **quiicast.com/caster** (spell it out slowly: Q-U-I-I-C-A-S-T dot com, then "slash caster").
3. They click the big **Start sharing my screen** button and choose to share their **Entire Screen** when their browser asks.
4. They read you the 4-digit code that appears on their screen.
5. You go to **quiicast.com/receiver** on your own computer, type in the code, and their screen appears on yours.

Because the connection is peer-to-peer, you're seeing their actual screen in real time, so you can say "click the one that says Settings, top right" instead of guessing where the cursor probably is. If they need to move the mouse somewhere themselves, you can talk them there visually instead of over a phone held at arm's length pointed at the monitor.

## What to know before you start

- The code expires after a few minutes and stops working once the person closes the tab, so there's nothing left running afterward for them to worry about.
- If it's just diagnosing a settings issue, that's normally enough — you're watching, not controlling their computer.
- On a slow connection, dropping the quality preset (720p is usually still plenty readable) can make a laggy older laptop feel much smoother to watch.
- Nothing gets installed on their machine — if they're at all wary after hearing about tech-support scams, that's a reasonable thing to point out: there's no software to download, ever.

## FAQ

**Is this safe? Can someone else see my parent's screen?**
Only whoever has the exact 4-digit code, which is different every session and expires after a short time. There's no persistent link or account that stays valid afterward.

**Can I control their mouse and fix it myself instead of talking them through it?**
No — QuiiCast is view-only, not remote control. For diagnosing settings issues, reading error messages, and giving directions, that's usually enough; for something that genuinely requires you to click things on their machine, you'd need a different, more heavyweight tool (and more trust, since that kind of access is a bigger step).

**What if they're not comfortable using a computer at all?**
Having them read the code out loud is the only technical step required on their end beyond opening a browser and clicking one button — for most people, that's a low enough bar even if they're generally not confident with technology.
