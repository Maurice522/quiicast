Zoom, Google Meet and Microsoft Teams are excellent at being meeting platforms. That's also the reason they're awkward for a very small job. Starting a meeting means a link, a lobby, camera and microphone prompts, sometimes a sign-in, and a few seconds of "can you see my screen?" before anything useful happens. For a ninety-second "look at this error," the setup can easily outlast the task.

This is a decision guide, not a pitch. In many situations the meeting platform is the better choice, and the point is to tell the two cases apart quickly.

## Three questions that settle it

**1. How many people, and do they need to talk to each other?** If it's two to five people watching one screen while you narrate over a call that already exists, a quick share is enough. If it's a conversation with cameras, breakout groups or a presenter handover, you want a meeting platform.

**2. Will you need it afterward?** Recordings, transcripts, chat history and shared files all belong to meeting platforms. A browser-only share keeps none of that, by design: nothing is stored once the tab closes.

**3. Is it one moment or a recurring thing?** A recurring weekly meeting earns its calendar invite. A one-off glance doesn't.

## Rules of thumb

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Walking a relative through a settings screen | Quick share |
| Standing weekly team meeting | Meeting platform |
| Showing one teammate a failing build | Quick share |
| Client presentation with slides and Q&A | Meeting platform |
| Needing a recording for people who missed it | Meeting platform |
| Interview where you want to see someone's own editor | Quick share, plus your normal call for audio |
| Fixing a setting on someone else's computer yourself | Remote-desktop tool, not either of these |

## What a quick share actually looks like

The sender opens [the cast page](/caster), clicks start, chooses what to show, and reads out a four-digit code. Each viewer opens [the receive page](/receiver) and types it. No account is needed, nothing is installed, and the video travels directly between browsers. Anything with a modern browser can watch, including a phone. [How it works](/how-it-works) has the details, and the [no-download comparison](/blog/screen-share-without-downloading) lays out what each tool requires.

## The honest limits

A quick share has no chat, no recording, no participant management, and a ceiling of five viewers. It can't take control of the other machine, and sending from an iPhone isn't possible from a browser. If any of those is a requirement, don't try to stretch it. The better habit is to keep both tools available and pick by the three questions above.

If the task is small enough that the meeting would take longer than the thing you're showing, that's the case a browser share exists for.
